Is 15,000 satellites real?

Yes, as a pending authorization request. On September 16, 2025, SpaceX filed FCC application SAT-LOA-20250916-00282 (Call Sign S00735) seeking authority for a new constellation of up to 15,000 NGSO satellites dedicated to direct-to-cell service, separate from and in addition to its Gen2 broadband authorization[1][2]. SpaceX amended the application on November 25, 2025 (SAT-AMD-20251125-00339), and the FCC placed it on public notice in GN Docket No. 25-340[1]. The FCC's notice describes an MSS system of up to 15,000 satellites operating at 326–335 km[1] — deliberately low, trading continuous drag compensation and ~5-year satellite lifetimes for link-budget margin, smaller beam footprints, and sub-50 ms latency. The application proposes SCS operations in the United States on the PCS G-block, the H-block, and the unpaired AWS-3 block, plus MSS operations on the AWS-4 (2 GHz) spectrum acquired from EchoStar[1][3].

Two cautions. First, 15,000 is a requested ceiling, not a delivered plan: at SpaceX's target Starship cadence the full fleet is a roughly-2030s proposition, and sustaining it requires replacing ~3,000 satellites per year indefinitely given ~5-year orbital lifetimes. Second, parts of the application remain contested: the FCC's April 23, 2026 MSS order (DA 26-398) reaffirmed that the 1.6/2.4 GHz "Big LEO" bands SpaceX also requested are not available for an additional MSS system, while confirming exclusive 2 GHz rights flow through the EchoStar transaction[3].

For context on the current fleet: the first-generation D2C constellation is fully deployed at roughly 650 satellites — V2 Mini-class buses carrying an added D2C payload — out of a much larger broadband constellation whose satellites cannot serve phones at all[4][5].

Will all 15,000 serve the continental US?

No — and orbital mechanics make a US-only constellation impossible. The FCC application itself proposes service "in the United States and outside of the United States"[3]. Satellites in low-Earth orbit at ~53°-class inclinations circle the entire planet roughly every 90 minutes; any individual spacecraft spends only a small percentage of its time over the continental US. That is precisely why the constellation must be so large: to keep hundreds of satellites usefully positioned over any given market at every moment, you need many thousands distributed globally. At a full 15,000-satellite fleet, the number over CONUS at any instant would be on the order of several hundred, with the remainder simultaneously serving SpaceX's international carrier partners — Kyivstar (Ukraine), Optus/Telstra (Australia), KDDI (Japan), Airtel Africa, Virgin Media O2 (UK), Deutsche Telekom (Europe), and others — plus oceans and unserved territory[4][6].

The regulatory and spectrum layers, however, are country-specific. In the US, SpaceX will operate on spectrum it owns outright (AWS-4, H-block, AWS-3) plus the leased T-Mobile G-block[1][3]. Abroad, the same satellites operate on each partner carrier's licensed spectrum under each country's rules — supplemented going forward by the globally harmonized 2 GHz S-band rights acquired from EchoStar, which SpaceX says carry priority ITU rights allowing consistent international scaling subject to national approvals[6]. The practical consequence: the US is the only market where SpaceX controls the full spectrum stack, which is why the fourth-carrier ambition is a US story while international markets remain a partnership and wholesale play.

How large is the D2C antenna array?

The current-generation D2C payload (flying on V2 Mini buses) carries a phased array of approximately 25 m² dedicated to cellular communication[5]. For the next-generation dedicated satellites, SpaceX's SVP of Starlink Michael Nicolls stated at MWC 2026 that they carry a phased array five times the size of the first-generation hardware — implying roughly a 125 m²-class aperture — delivering four times the bandwidth per beam and about 20× the link performance, supporting over 100 Gbps of downlink and 50 Gbps of uplink per satellite, 20×20 MHz beams, MIMO, and the 3GPP Release-19 NR-NTN standard, with claimed user speeds up to 150 Mbps and commercial launch targeted for mid-2027[4][6][7]. SpaceX's own materials describe the next-generation satellites as supporting "thousands of spatial beams"[8]. Exact antenna dimensions and element counts are not published; SpaceX discloses power and emissions envelopes to the FCC, not antenna blueprints, so the ~125 m² figure is a derived estimate from the stated 5× ratio.

For calibration against the competing architecture: AST SpaceMobile's first-generation BlueBirds (1–5) carry ~64 m² (693 sq ft) arrays, and its Block 2 BlueBirds unfold to approximately 223 m² (2,400 sq ft) — the largest commercial phased arrays ever deployed in LEO — supporting up to 10 GHz of processing bandwidth across ~2,000 coverage cells per satellite[9][10][11]. AST buys tighter beams with a monolithic giant antenna and pays in satellite mass (~6,100 kg for BlueBird 6, versus ~800 kg for a D2C-capable V2 Mini) and per-unit complexity[5][10]; SpaceX buys capacity with a mid-sized aperture multiplied by fleet count and launch cadence. Same link-budget physics, opposite industrial bets.
